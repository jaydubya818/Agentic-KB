STORY 1 — SCALING WORKDAY’S INTERNAL AI PLATFORM

Best for: AI Platform Strategy | Scaling | Adoption | Architecture | Engineering Leadership

Likelihood: EXTREMELY HIGH | 90 Seconds

SITUATION

“At Workday, engineering teams across the company were beginning to experiment with LLMs, copilots, and intelligent agents.

The challenge was that hundreds of teams were independently building similar capabilities, including agent frameworks, enterprise retrieval, tool integrations, authentication, evaluation, and observability.

Without a shared platform, we risked duplicated engineering effort, fragmented architectures, inconsistent security, and AI solutions that weren’t production-ready.”

TASK

“My responsibility was to lead the engineering organization responsible for establishing a shared, governed AI platform that could scale across Workday.

We needed to balance rapid innovation, developer experience, security, reliability, and organizational adoption.”

ACTION

“I led a 12-engineer organization responsible for developing two complementary platforms.

Workbench became our governed agent platform, providing agent orchestration, execution harnesses, MCP integrations, reusable skills, evaluation, observability, and security controls.

Hopper became our enterprise knowledge platform, providing permission-aware RAG and trusted retrieval across organizational information.

I established the engineering strategy, roadmap, architecture direction, ownership boundaries, and production-readiness expectations.

I also partnered with Product, Security, Infrastructure, and engineering leaders to identify high-value workflows and establish reusable development patterns.

One important architectural decision was to centralize common platform capabilities while allowing individual engineering teams to own their specific agents and workflows.

We also introduced developer enablement, evaluation standards, and controlled rollouts to support adoption.”

RESULT

“We scaled the platform from six pilot teams to more than 300 engineering teams, supporting approximately 3,000 engineers.

The platform enabled more than 25 customer-facing agents and 50 internal agents.

We improved evaluated agent task success from 15% to 65% across a measured cohort, while reducing cost per agent run by 45%.”

LESSON

“The biggest lesson was that AI platforms become enterprise infrastructure much faster than expected.

Once hundreds of teams depend on them, success is no longer just about model capability.

It’s about trust, reliability, adoption, governance, and measurable business impact.”

KEY TAKEAWAY

“The goal wasn’t to build another agent. It was to build a shared platform that hundreds of teams could safely build agents on.”

STORY 2 — WORKBENCH STABILIZATION AND PRODUCTION READINESS

Best for: Failure | Ownership | Reliability | Accountability | Engineering Excellence

Likelihood: VERY HIGH | 90 Seconds

SITUATION

“During Workbench’s beta, we encountered reliability issues that exposed gaps in our release and production-readiness practices.

We were moving quickly, but our operating model hadn’t matured at the same pace as platform adoption.

Some agent deployments were failing in staging and production, and users occasionally experienced workflows that didn’t return useful responses.

For an internal AI platform trying to earn engineers’ trust, that was a serious problem.”

TASK

“As the engineering leader, I needed to stabilize the platform, restore confidence, and establish stronger production standards without eliminating the experimentation that made the platform valuable.”

ACTION

“I took ownership of the issue as a leadership and operating-model gap, rather than blaming individual engineers.

I temporarily shifted engineering priorities away from lower-value feature expansion and toward platform stabilization.

We introduced several improvements.

First, mandatory staging validation. Critical workflows had to be validated against acceptance criteria before production release.

Second, automated smoke and regression testing. We validated the core user experience, agent orchestration, retrieval, tool integrations, and backend services.

Third, observability and deployment visibility. We improved execution tracing, monitoring, and failure diagnosis.

Fourth, explicit ownership. Every critical defect had an assigned owner, severity, and resolution plan.

Fifth, release-readiness criteria. We established clearer expectations around validation evidence, rollback, and production support.

Most importantly, I changed our definition of done.

Done no longer meant code merged. It meant validated, observable, recoverable, and production-ready.”

RESULT

“We stabilized the critical workflows and established a more reliable release process.

The team gained stronger operational discipline, and stakeholders had clearer visibility into release readiness and production risks.

More importantly, we changed the engineering culture around reliability.

Production readiness became part of feature delivery rather than something addressed after deployment.”

LESSON

“The failure wasn’t experimenting quickly.

The failure was allowing adoption to outpace production maturity.

As a leader, I learned that the engineering standard must evolve as a platform becomes critical infrastructure.”

KEY TAKEAWAY

“Code complete is not platform complete.”

STORY 3 — BUILD VS. ADOPT VS. PARTNER

Best for: Technical Judgment | Investment Prioritization | ROI | Architecture Tradeoffs

Likelihood: VERY HIGH | 60–90 Seconds

SITUATION

“As Workday’s AI platform expanded, we faced constant decisions about whether to build new capabilities internally or adopt existing technologies.

The AI ecosystem was evolving rapidly, with new models, frameworks, developer tools, and infrastructure products emerging almost weekly.

Building everything ourselves would slow delivery and increase maintenance costs.

But adopting everything externally could reduce control over critical platform capabilities.”

TASK

“My responsibility was to establish a disciplined framework for technical investment decisions, balancing speed, differentiation, security, reliability, and long-term ownership.”

ACTION

“I introduced a Build / Adopt / Partner framework.

For each capability, we evaluated five factors.

First, differentiation. Does building this capability create a meaningful competitive or organizational advantage?

Second, governance. Do we need direct control over security, permissions, data, or execution policies?

Third, maturity. Does an existing technology already solve the problem reliably?

Fourth, integration and flexibility. Can we adopt the capability without creating excessive vendor lock-in?

Fifth, total cost of ownership. What will it cost to build, operate, maintain, upgrade, and support over time?

My general approach was to own the differentiating platform capabilities and governance contracts while adopting mature, replaceable infrastructure where appropriate.

That allowed us to focus engineering capacity on the capabilities that mattered most.”

RESULT

“In one decision, adopting an existing solution rather than building an equivalent internally avoided approximately six engineer-months of development effort.

We redirected that capacity toward higher-priority platform capabilities.

The framework also improved architectural discussions because decisions were based on explicit business and technical tradeoffs.”

LESSON

“Build-versus-buy isn’t simply a technology decision.

It’s an economic and architectural decision about where the organization needs long-term control.”

KEY TAKEAWAY

“Strong engineering leadership is as much about deciding what not to build as deciding what to build.”

STORY 4 — DEVELOPING A SENIOR ENGINEER INTO AN ARCHITECT (Garrett Nelson) 

Best for: Coaching | Leadership Development | Delegation | Technical Leadership

Likelihood: VERY HIGH | 90 Seconds

SITUATION

“At Workday, I had a senior engineer who was technically exceptional.

He had strong architecture instincts, deep infrastructure knowledge, and credibility with other engineers.

But as our AI platform expanded, we needed him to move beyond individual technical contributions and begin influencing architecture decisions across multiple teams.”

TASK

“My goal was to help him grow from a strong Senior Engineer into an Architect, expanding his impact through technical leadership, cross-team influence, and architectural ownership.”

ACTION

“I approached his development through context, ownership, visibility, and coaching.

First, I brought him into roadmap and platform strategy discussions earlier, while we were still defining the problem.

Second, I gave him ownership of broader architectural decisions involving Workbench, Hopper, MCP integrations, developer tooling, and platform scalability.

Third, I encouraged him to lead architecture reviews and document decisions through technical design documents and architecture decision records.

Fourth, I coached him on communicating technical tradeoffs, influencing other engineers, and connecting architecture decisions to business outcomes.

Finally, I deliberately stepped back from certain technical discussions so he could establish himself as a trusted architectural leader.

I remained available for coaching, but I wanted him to develop independent decision-making authority.”

RESULT

“He grew into an Architect role with broader platform influence.

His technical judgment became more visible across the organization, and he helped establish clearer architecture standards and technical direction.

It also reduced the organization’s dependency on me for individual technical decisions.”

LESSON

“Developing exceptional engineers isn’t about giving them more difficult coding assignments.

It’s about helping them scale their judgment, influence, and impact across the organization.”

KEY TAKEAWAY

“I don’t scale by making every technical decision. I scale by developing engineers who can make great decisions without me.”

STORY 5 — MISSION CONTROL: AUTONOMOUS SOFTWARE DELIVERY

Best for: AI-First Engineering | Technical Credibility | Innovation | Agentic Systems | Architecture

Likelihood: HIGH | 90 Seconds

SITUATION

“Outside Workday, I was experimenting with multiple AI coding agents across different repositories.

I noticed that as agents became more capable, the bottleneck wasn’t generating code anymore.

It was coordinating execution, managing permissions, tracking progress, validating results, and maintaining human oversight.

Adding more agents was actually increasing the coordination burden.”

TASK

“I wanted to explore how autonomous engineering could scale without creating uncontrolled execution or requiring a human to supervise every action.

That became my open-source project, Mission Control.”

ACTION

“I designed Mission Control as a governed autonomous software delivery platform.

The architecture separates three responsibilities.

Humans define intent and retain consequential authority.

Agents perform bounded engineering tasks.

Deterministic systems enforce security, validation, and governance.

I implemented capabilities including:

Agent execution harnesses with scoped permissions.
Sandboxed execution and isolated development environments.
Durable workflow state and controlled task execution.
Independent verification, where agents cannot approve their own work.
Evidence-based quality gates.
Human approval for consequential decisions such as merging and releasing software.

The platform follows a lifecycle from Mission → Planning → Agent Execution → Verification → Evidence → Pull Request → Human Acceptance.”

RESULT

“I demonstrated an end-to-end workflow where agents execute engineering tasks and generate pull requests supported by independent verification evidence.

Mission Control also gives me a practical environment to experiment with agent orchestration, execution reliability, evaluation, governance, and AI-native engineering practices.

It keeps me close to implementation while allowing me to apply those lessons to engineering leadership.”

LESSON

“The biggest insight is that agent autonomy and agent authority are different things.

Agents can become increasingly capable without being given uncontrolled authority.”

KEY TAKEAWAY

“The goal isn’t autonomous coding. It’s governed autonomous software delivery.”

STORY 6 — CORNERSTONE: OPERATION STABILIZE

Best for: Operational Excellence | Enterprise Reliability | Cross-Functional Leadership | Crisis Management

Likelihood: HIGH | 90 Seconds

SITUATION

“At Cornerstone, we faced significant reliability and integration challenges across a portfolio of approximately 21 enterprise products and more than 50 integrations.

The products supported critical employee workflows across Recruiting, Onboarding, Learning, Performance, and Talent Management.

Because these systems had been developed independently, customers were experiencing issues with integration handoffs, data accuracy, permissions, and release quality.

Customer confidence was beginning to suffer.”

TASK

“I was asked to lead a 90-day stabilization initiative focused on restoring reliability, improving engineering quality, and establishing stronger ownership across the portfolio.”

ACTION

“I launched an initiative called Operation Stabilize.

First, I partnered with Product, Engineering, Support, Quality, and Operations to identify the highest-risk customer workflows and recurring production issues.

Second, I reorganized validation around end-to-end employee workflows, rather than isolated product modules.

Third, we strengthened automated regression testing, integration validation, data integrity checks, and release-readiness practices.

Fourth, I clarified ownership across product teams so issues couldn’t simply be passed between organizations.

Finally, I established regular executive reporting around critical defects, quality trends, operational risks, and stabilization progress.

The biggest cultural shift was moving from QA owning quality at the end to Engineering owning quality throughout the development lifecycle.”

RESULT

“We strengthened reliability across critical workflows and established a more disciplined engineering operating model.

Across our broader modernization efforts, we reduced manual validation by 60% and increased release throughput by 30%.

The initiative also improved cross-functional accountability and helped restore confidence in the platform.”

LESSON

“Operational excellence requires more than fixing defects.

It requires clear ownership, end-to-end accountability, measurable quality standards, and disciplined execution.”

KEY TAKEAWAY

“Quality isn’t simply an engineering metric. It’s customer trust.”
