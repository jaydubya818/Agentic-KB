1. CORNERSTONE ATS / RECRUITING CLOUD BUILD

Core Message

“I helped turn broad and ambiguous Talent Acquisition requirements into a focused strategy, scalable Engineering organization, complete MVP, and enterprise recruiting platform delivered in 23 months and 28 days.”

At Cornerstone, I was the Engineering Manager responsible for helping build our ATS / Recruiting product from the ground up.

This was not a small enhancement. It was a new enterprise Talent Acquisition platform with major business visibility, customer commitments, revenue impact, and a fixed delivery window.

We had:

Less than 24 months
100+ pages of vague and changing requirements
Hundreds of requested features
Contractual customer commitments
Significant integration and workflow complexity
A team that had to scale quickly

The strategic problem was not simply how to deliver the most features.

It was how to define the smallest complete recruiting operating model that customers could actually implement, adopt, operate, and extend.

That meant the MVP had to support the full recruiting journey:

Requisition creation → Approvals → Candidate pipeline → Hiring manager collaboration → Interviews → Feedback → Offers → Reporting → Permissions → Integrations → Onboarding handoff

I helped create structure around the ambiguity by separating the work into:

Core MVP workflows
Contractual commitments
High-risk integrations
Customer-specific requests
Product preferences
Launch requirements
Fast-follow enhancements

The key strategy decision was that workflow completeness took priority over feature breadth.

We deliberately deferred some valuable point capabilities so the first release could operate coherently end to end.

The strategy-building work was defining the minimum complete recruiting operating model customers could adopt.

The implementation work was translating that strategy into:

Phased workstreams
Milestones
Architecture boundaries
Team ownership
Integration sequencing
Quality and automation strategy
Environment planning across QA, Pilot, Stage, and Production
Release-readiness criteria
Recurring decision points
Customer-readiness risk visibility
Launch and adoption planning

I also helped scale the Engineering organization quickly to roughly 24 engineers, while keeping focus on ownership, delivery discipline, quality, and risk visibility.

My operating principle was:

“I would rather give stakeholders options early than surprises late.”

Result

We delivered Cornerstone Recruiting Cloud in 23 months and 28 days.

The product gave customers a complete recruiting workflow they could implement and run, rather than disconnected features that looked good individually but failed as an operating process.

The product was recognized as a Top HR Product and received a Gold Award for Best New Human Capital Management Solution. I was also named Employee of the Year among approximately 3,500 employees.

2. CORNERSTONE TALENT AND HCM  OPERATION STABILIZE


⸻
Core Message

“I converted a CEO-visible quality and reliability problem across roughly 21 Talent and HCM products into a portfolio implementation model with clear priorities, owners, operating mechanisms, validation discipline, and senior executive visibility.”

At Cornerstone, I spent 13 years leading Engineering across HCM and Talent products, including Recruiting, Onboarding, Learning, Performance, Talent Management, employee and manager self-service, integrations, Quality Engineering, and automation.

The problem was that we had roughly 21 interconnected products, but many had been built and validated as separate applications with different architectures, data models, APIs, automation coverage, and release-readiness standards.

Customers did not experience those systems separately.

They expected one connected employee lifecycle:

Candidate → New hire → Onboarding → Learning → Compliance → Performance → Talent Management → Mobility

The strategic decision was to stop managing the problem as a collection of independent defects and treat stability as an end-to-end portfolio outcome.

The implementation challenge was that the issues crossed:

Products
Teams
Integrations
Data flows
Ownership boundaries
Release processes
Support paths

No single team could resolve the complete problem independently.

The strategy-building work was defining how to prioritize the portfolio based on business and operational impact, not just defect count.

I helped establish shared priorities based on:

Customer impact
Workflow criticality
Frequency
Business risk
Data integrity
Integration dependencies
Support burden
Release confidence

The implementation work was turning that strategy into sustained operating mechanisms.

We implemented six mechanisms:

Customer-impact triage — separated normal defects from issues affecting trust, data integrity, compliance, reporting, permissions, workflow handoffs, and integration reliability.
Lifecycle operating model — organized the work around end-to-end employee and customer workflows, not internal product silos.
End-to-end validation — validated complete workflows across products, data, integrations, permissions, and downstream systems.
Automation and regression discipline — expanded coverage around the highest-risk and highest-volume workflows.
Release-readiness criteria — created clearer expectations for what had to be true before release, including validation evidence, ownership, known risks, and support readiness.
Executive visibility — gave leaders visibility into highest-risk workflows, named owners, dependency risks, deferred work, decisions required, and measurable progress.

The tradeoff was that some planned feature development moved out so the organization could address systemic reliability and ownership problems.

That was the right decision because continuing to add features on an unstable operating model would have increased support burden, customer escalation pressure, rework, and loss of trust.

Result

The result was stronger stability, clearer ownership, reduced escalation pressure, improved release confidence, stronger end-to-end validation, and a more durable operating model for managing quality across the Talent and HCM portfolio.

David Kim Relevance

This maps to strategy building and implementation because the work was not simply fixing defects.

It was turning a broad operational problem into a practical implementation model:

Business risk → portfolio priorities → ownership → operating mechanisms → validation → executive visibility → measurable improvement

3. WORKDAY AI-NATIVE PLATFORM AND WORKBENCH LEADERSHIP

Best Interviewer Fit

Natalie Haywood — Organizational leverage + measurable platform impact
David Kim — AI strategy + implementation model for Talent Operations

⸻

Core Message

“I helped move Workday from isolated AI experiments to shared, governed, production-ready AI capabilities.”

At Workday, I lead a 12-engineer senior-heavy AI Engineering organization building shared AI-native platforms used across more than 300 scrum teams and approximately 3,000 engineers.

The organizational problem was that AI demand was growing quickly. Teams wanted to build agents, copilots, knowledge assistants, enterprise search, workflow automation, testing agents, developer agents, and customer-facing AI experiences.

Without a shared platform, each team would have recreated its own agent framework, retrieval layer, tool integrations, evaluation model, observability, permission model, SDK, governance process, and production-readiness approach.

That would have created duplicated work, inconsistent architecture, uneven quality, security and compliance risk, weak auditability, and impressive demos that were not ready for production.

My role was to turn that broad and ambiguous AI demand into a scalable platform strategy and execution model.

We built Workbench as the governed AI platform and agent harness, and Hopper as the enterprise RAG knowledge platform that provides trusted, source-backed context.

The key leadership decision was to centralize the repeated, high-risk platform capabilities while allowing domain teams to own their specific workflows and user experiences.

That required influence across Engineering leaders, Product teams, architects, Security, infrastructure, and senior stakeholders.

I had to frame the decision not as “central platform versus team autonomy,” but as a tradeoff between short-term local speed and long-term organizational leverage, governance, reliability, and production readiness.

The recommendation was to centralize the foundation that every AI team would otherwise need to rebuild:

Authentication
Retrieval
Tool use
Evaluation
Observability
Governance
Release-readiness patterns

while keeping differentiated business workflows with the teams closest to the users.

Result

The impact was measurable. Workbench and Hopper support:

300+ scrum teams
3,000 engineers
25+ customer-facing agents
50+ internal agents

We also avoided more than 10,000 hours of duplicated Engineering effort.

I describe that carefully as a conservative avoided-effort estimate based on common platform capabilities teams would otherwise have needed to design, implement, validate, and maintain independently — not as direct productivity savings or guaranteed labor reduction.

The biggest result was not simply the number of agents.

The larger impact was establishing a repeatable operating model that helped teams move from isolated AI experiments to governed, production-ready systems.

Core Message

“I replaced general feedback with explicit weekly expectations, measurable evidence, direct coaching, and consistent follow-through.”

I had an engineer whose performance concerns involved inconsistent ownership, delivery follow-through, documentation, communication, responsiveness, and visibility.

The risk was allowing the situation to remain subjective.

If I kept giving general feedback like “be more proactive” or “own the work more clearly,” the employee could interpret that one way, the team could experience it another way, and I would not have a fair basis to evaluate whether the pattern was actually improving.

So I moved from general feedback to a structured coaching plan.

I gave direct, evidence-based feedback and established:

Clear weekly outcomes
Communication expectations
Review points
Documentation requirements
Delivery commitments
Validation expectations
Measures of sustained improvement

I also clarified the support I would provide:

Help with prioritization
Clarifying acceptance criteria
Reviewing plans
Removing blockers
Providing coaching and feedback

But I was also clear that support did not remove accountability.

The employee still owned the outcome.

Each week, we reviewed actual evidence:

What was committed
What was completed
What was communicated
What was documented
What was validated
What follow-through occurred
Where the same pattern repeated

That moved the conversation away from intent and into observable behavior.

Result

The result was that he turned it around.

His ownership became more consistent, his communication became more proactive, his documentation improved, and he started raising blockers earlier instead of letting them become delivery surprises.

The weekly structure made improvement visible and evidence-based.

It gave him a fair path to succeed, rebuilt team confidence, and showed that support and accountability can work together without lowering the standard.

Missed commitments
Late blockers
Repeated managerial follow-up
Incomplete ownership
Temporary improvement followed by regression
Teammates absorbing execution gaps

The difficult part was that the engineer had demonstrated the ability to meet expectations when receiving close attention.

The issue was that the performance did not sustain without repeated managerial intervention.

I gave direct, evidence-based feedback and clarified the required behaviors:

Realistic commitments
Proactive communication
Early escalation
Consistent engagement
Complete validation
Follow-through without repeated prompting

I used observable examples rather than assumptions about intent or character.

My message was:

“You have demonstrated that you can meet these expectations. The issue is that the performance and ownership have not been consistent or sustained.”

I evaluated whether the root cause was skill, workload, context, dependencies, role fit, or accountability.

I provided support with:

Planning
Prioritization
Work decomposition
Communication
Blocker removal
Expectation clarity

When coaching did not produce sustained improvement, I moved into a structured, time-bound performance process with:

Measurable objectives
Weekly checkpoints
Clear success criteria
Documented evidence
Explicit consequences

I partnered with my manager and Human Resources to ensure the process was fair, consistent, documented, and respectful.

After the transition, coordination overhead decreased, rework decreased, ownership became clearer, and delivery became more predictable.

“I resolved a conflict between Engineering, Product, business stakeholders, and customer-facing teams after discovering that requested capabilities were tied to contractual commitments that had not been communicated to Engineering during planning.”

The business believed Engineering was blocking customer needs.

Engineering believed scope was being added without acknowledging delivery consequences.

I discovered that several requests were tied to contractual commitments that had not been surfaced during planning.

Neither side had the full picture.

I moved the conversation away from blame and toward explicit options, tradeoffs, and decision rights.

I separated:

Contractual commitments
Core MVP workflows
Product preferences
Customer-specific requests
High-value fast follows
Future enhancements
Available capacity
Architecture dependencies
Quality risks
Date constraints

I then presented three options:

Include all requested scope and accept delay and greater quality risk.
Protect the launch by limiting the release to contractual requirements and core workflows.
Exchange scope by adding contractual requirements and removing lower-value planned work.

My recommendation was the third option.

I reframed the conversation from:

“Engineering is saying no.”

to:

“What are we willing to delay, remove, reduce, or risk to include this?”

I also clarified decision rights.

Engineering owned estimates, architecture, feasibility, and quality risk.

Product and business leaders owned customer prioritization, commercial commitments, and final scope decisions.

Result

We aligned on a credible release containing contractual coverage, core customer value, and critical workflows.

Lower-priority work moved to a sequenced fast-follow roadmap.

The conflict de-escalated, and contractual commitments became an explicit input to future planning.

9. TEAM CONFLICT AND BEHAVIORAL ACCOUNTABILITY — BRANDON

Best Interviewer Fit

John Pham — candor · trust · behavioral accountability · high standards

⸻

Core Message

“When conflict arises within a team, I try to be impartial about the diagnosis, but I am not neutral about the standard.”

I had a technically strong senior engineer whose interactions were creating conflict.

He was raising legitimate concerns, but the way he communicated was becoming dismissive, defensive, and difficult for others to engage with.

The tension was that I did not want to dismiss the issue just because the delivery was poor.

At the same time, I could not allow technical strength to excuse behavior that reduced trust, collaboration, and team effectiveness.

I used a framework I call CLEAR:

Clarify the conflict — understand what is actually happening and separate facts from assumptions.
Listen to all sides — understand each person’s perspective before assigning blame.
Examine the system — look for unclear ownership, decision-right ambiguity, process gaps, or competing priorities.
Align on expectations — give direct feedback and define the behavioral standard.
Reinforce accountability and results — follow up and confirm that trust, collaboration, and execution improve.

I met separately with him and the people involved.

What I found was that there were two issues happening at the same time.

His communication style was not acceptable, but some of his concerns were valid. There were real gaps around ownership, decision rights, and response expectations between teams.

I separated the validity of the concern from the acceptability of the behavior.

I gave him direct, specific feedback. I did not just say, “communicate better.”

I walked through examples and explained the impact:

His code-review comments were becoming harder for people to engage with.
His escalations were creating defensiveness.
His communication was reducing his influence.
People were starting to work around him instead of with him.

I made the expected change clear:

Challenge the work without dismissing the person.
Make feedback specific and actionable.
Raise concerns constructively.
Escalate only when the normal ownership path is not working.
Treat collaboration as part of the senior Engineering role.

At the same time, I worked with the teams to clarify ownership, decision rights, and response expectations so the same friction did not keep recurring.

When the behavior did not change consistently at first, I had a more direct accountability conversation.

I made it clear that strong technical output was not enough if his overall impact made the team less effective.

Senior engineers are expected to raise the quality of decisions and the quality of collaboration around them.

Result

The result was that his approach improved.

Code reviews became more constructive, partner-team issues were resolved more directly, unnecessary escalations decreased, and his technical influence increased because people were more willing to engage with him.

John Relevance

This story maps directly to candor, judgment, accountability, and talent density.

I did not avoid the hard feedback.

I did not reduce the situation to a personality issue.

I tested whether the concern was valid, fixed the system gaps, and still held the behavioral standard.

That is how I think high-performance teams should operate.

Strong people should be encouraged to challenge problems directly, but they also need to make the team stronger through how they communicate and collaborate.

Strong Lines

“I am impartial about the diagnosis, but I am not neutral about the standard.”
