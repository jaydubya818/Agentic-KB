Workday Workbench (AI-Native Platform), Team and Leadership

Core Message

At Workday, I lead a 12-engineer organization building a shared AI-native engineering platform designed to support more than 300 scrum teams and 3,000 engineers.

My responsibility is not simply to build individual agents. It is to create the shared agent harness, trusted knowledge layer, tool integrations, governance, evaluation, and production-readiness capabilities that allow teams to build AI experiences without independently recreating the same foundation.

⸻

Opening Hook

The central problem I am solving is not how to build more AI agents. It is how to prevent hundreds of engineering teams from building hundreds of disconnected AI stacks.

Across Workday, teams wanted to create copilots, knowledge assistants, development and testing agents, code-review agents, workflow automation, and customer-facing AI experiences.

The opportunity was significant, but so was the organizational risk.

Without a shared platform, each team could have built its own agent framework, retrieval layer, authentication model, tool integrations, evaluation approach, observability stack, and governance process.

That would have created duplicated engineering effort, fragmented architecture, inconsistent security, uneven quality, and impressive demonstrations that were not ready for production.

⸻

My Responsibility

My responsibility was to turn that broad AI demand into a scalable platform strategy, architecture, operating model, and execution plan.

I lead the teams responsible for two primary platforms:

Workbench: the governed AI platform, agent harness, and control plane behind our AI engineering experiences.
Hopper: the enterprise knowledge-ingestion and retrieval platform that gives agents trusted, source-backed internal context.

My broader scope includes MCP and tool integrations, reusable agentic skills, deterministic automation, authentication and permissions, evaluations, observability, workflow orchestration, human approvals, and production readiness.

My goal was not to centralize every agent or prevent experimentation.

It was to centralize the common, undifferentiated capabilities every team would otherwise need to build, secure, test, deploy, and operate independently.

That allowed domain teams to focus on the customer and business problems that differentiated their products.

⸻

Action One: Define the Platform Strategy

So, the first thing I did was redefine the problem from building individual AI features to creating an enterprise platform.

I partnered with Product, engineering leaders, architects, Security, Infrastructure, Developer Experience, application teams, and senior executives to define what Workbench needed to become.

I positioned Workbench not as another chatbot, but as the shared platform and agent harness behind multiple AI experiences.

I converted that vision into:

Platform phases and milestones
Architecture decisions
Ownership boundaries
Implementation plans
Release criteria
Production-readiness expectations

The foundation included agent orchestration, workflow state, enterprise retrieval, tool integrations, authentication, permission-aware execution, human approvals, evaluations, and observability.

This created a shared architectural direction while allowing teams to retain ownership of their domain-specific experiences.

⸻

Action Two: Build the Harness and Trusted Knowledge Layer

The second action was building the complete system around the model.

One principle guided our approach:

The model provides intelligence, but the harness determines whether that intelligence can be applied safely and reliably.

Model access alone was not enough. Teams also needed trusted context, approved tools, workflow state, execution boundaries, validation, human oversight, observability, and recovery behavior.

Workbench provided the agent harness and control plane. Hopper provided the trusted knowledge layer.

Hopper created shared capabilities for approved-source ingestion, document processing, metadata enrichment, keyword and vector indexing, hybrid retrieval, filtering, citations, provenance, retrieval evaluation, and freshness monitoring.

The distinction was simple:

Workbench determines what an agent can do and how its execution is governed. Hopper provides the trusted context the agent needs to decide what to say or do.

Together, they created a foundation that was grounded, permission-aware, observable, evaluated, and governed.

⸻

Action Three: Separate Reasoning From Execution

The third major decision was separating work that required AI reasoning from work that should remain deterministic.

I did not want teams invoking an LLM for every operation simply because AI was available.

I used LLMs for ambiguity, reasoning, planning, interpretation, and synthesis.

I used APIs, SDKs, CLI commands, and scripts for predictable actions such as retrieving build status, creating a branch, updating a known field, running validation, or triggering an approved workflow.

My operating principle was:

Use AI where judgment is required. Use deterministic systems where predictability is required.

This made repeatable operations easier to test, secure, audit, and operate reliably, while also reducing unnecessary latency, cost, and failure risk.

⸻

Action Four: Establish Production Discipline

The fourth action was moving the organization from impressive demonstrations to production-ready systems.

In AI, the demonstration is usually the easy part.

The difficult work is creating something that is trusted, observable, evaluated, permission-aware, secure, auditable, recoverable, and durable enough for production.

We strengthened:

Agent and retrieval evaluations
Observability and feedback loops
Security review and auditability
Human-in-the-loop controls
Critical-flow and staging validation
Error handling and recovery
Release-readiness criteria

Teams still had room to experiment. But once an agent interacted with production systems, internal tools, sensitive data, or customer workflows, it needed common standards for permissions, evaluation, security, observability, and accountability.

My goal was not to let governance slow experimentation.

It was to create a clear path from experimentation to trusted production use.

⸻

Action Five: Lead Through Context and Ownership

Because I lead a senior-heavy organization, I did not want to become the architecture or decision bottleneck.

I gave architects, technical leads, and senior engineers:

The business problem
The user pain
The expected outcome
The technical constraints
The risks and dependencies
The tradeoffs
Clear decision rights

I then expected them to own the detailed solution and remain accountable for the result.

I led through context, clarity, ownership, and accountability—not micromanagement.

That allowed senior engineers to exercise judgment, influence architecture, guide other teams, and become organizational force multipliers.

⸻

Result

Workbench evolved into a reusable AI platform foundation that teams across Workday could build upon.

The broader platform supports an ecosystem that includes more than:

25 customer-facing agents
50 internal agents
300 engineering scrum teams
3,000 engineers

The platform gave teams a more consistent and governed approach to building agents, accessing trusted knowledge, connecting to approved tools, managing permissions, validating behavior, observing execution, and applying human oversight.

It also prevented teams from repeatedly rebuilding the same foundational capabilities.

We conservatively estimated that the platform avoided more than 10,000 hours of duplicated engineering effort across authentication, orchestration, retrieval, tool connectivity, evaluations, observability, security integration, deployment patterns, and production readiness.

That was an avoided-effort estimate, rather than a direct measurement of 10,000 individual productivity hours.

The broader outcomes included:

Less duplicated engineering work
Stronger architectural alignment
Greater platform reuse
Clearer ownership
Better security and governance
More consistent quality
A faster path from experimentation to production

⸻

Leadership Lesson

This experience reinforced that model capability is only one part of an enterprise AI system.

The model may provide intelligence, but the platform determines whether that intelligence can be used safely, repeatedly, and reliably at scale.

It also reinforced that some of the greatest platform value is invisible.

The value is not only the capabilities users see. It is also the thousands of hours teams do not spend rebuilding authentication, retrieval, tools, evaluations, observability, security, and governance.

My broader leadership lesson was:

Do not scale AI by multiplying isolated agents. Scale it by creating a trusted foundation that makes successful patterns reusable.

Transition Into Portfolio and Resource Planning

Portfolio and Resource Planning

Portfolio and resource planning are major parts of my current role, but I do not view them as something that happens only during quarterly planning. I view them as continuous decision-making.

I lead a 12-engineer organization, and we consistently have more valuable work than available capacity.

At any given time, I may be balancing production stability, new user-facing capabilities, platform improvements, retrieval quality, CI/CD investments, enterprise data sources, security requirements, and executive stakeholder requests.

My responsibility is not to tell every stakeholder yes.

My responsibility is to make the capacity constraint and tradeoffs explicit, recommend a path, and help the organization commit to a decision.

When priorities compete, I make the consequences of each option visible. That includes the business impact, engineering capacity required, delivery timeline, critical dependencies, operational risk, and the existing work that would need to move.

I do not present a neutral list of options and ask leadership to solve the problem for me. I provide a clear recommendation, explain why I believe it is the best path, and identify the risk we are choosing to accept.

A good example was a situation where we had several legitimate priorities, but enough capacity to deliver only a subset of them safely.

Rather than spreading the team thinly across everything, I made our actual available capacity visible. I separated non-discretionary reliability and operational work from optional roadmap work and presented stakeholders with explicit choices.

My recommendation protected the most important business outcome and our critical operational commitments while moving lower-value functionality into a sequenced and dated follow-up plan.

We did not say that we were going to “balance everything.”

We made a clear decision about what would be funded now, what would move, and what risk we were willing to accept.

That is how I approach portfolio leadership.

When everything is priority one, the real priority becomes whoever escalates the loudest. My job is to prevent that by creating an explicit and defensible order of operations.

I am comfortable changing the plan when the facts change. What I am not comfortable doing is pretending that the original scope, date, capacity, and risk remain unchanged after we introduce a new priority.

The common theme in my approach is that I create clarity around the outcome, real capacity, dependencies, available options, and risks.

I then make a recommendation and help the organization commit to the decision.

I do not want teams silently absorbing additional work or leaders discovering the cost of a decision only after a commitment has already been missed.

Integration patterns

Release-readiness standards

Validated implementations using a shared capability × conservative independent implementation effort avoided.
